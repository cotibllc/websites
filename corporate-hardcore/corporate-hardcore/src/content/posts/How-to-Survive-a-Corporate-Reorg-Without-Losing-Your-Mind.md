The reorg email arrived on a Thursday. Not Friday at 4:47 PM, which would have been honest. Thursday, mid-morning, subject line: Exciting News About Our Structure Going Forward.

I've been here eighteen years and I still read those subject lines the same way I read weather alerts. Not for information. For clues about what's already been decided.

This is not a guide from someone who survived one reorg. It's notes from someone who has documented several. The incidents blur together after a while. The pattern doesn't.

---

## The Announcement

The announcement will say this is about positioning the organization for growth. It will mention cross-functional alignment. It will use the phrase better serve our customers at least once. Someone will be named Chief of something new. Several people will not be named at all.

I documented the last one at [Synergy Corp](https://www.corphardcore.com/blog/the-reorg). The org chart had seventeen boxes. Four had names. The rest said TBD.

TBD is doing a lot of work in a reorg announcement. It means the decisions have been made but the communication strategy hasn't caught up. Or the decisions haven't been made and they're holding the ambiguity together until something firms up.

Both are possible. Both have happened here.

---

## What the Email Actually Says

There is the email. And then there is what the email says.

The email says: We are streamlining operations to better align with strategic priorities.

What the email says: Headcount is being reduced and the remaining headcount will absorb the work.

I've read this email before. The [path forward email](https://www.corphardcore.com/blog/record-growth-leaner-operations) is its own genre. It arrives after a quarter where revenue was up, and the explanation for why belts must tighten anyway requires a slide deck to follow. The reorg email is a cousin of that one. Same structure. Fewer numbers. More words like opportunity and evolution.

The useful skill is reading the gap between what is said and what is meant. Not as cynicism. As accuracy.

---

## What to Do in the First Week

Do not send the email asking where you land in the new structure. Not yet.

The people who know aren't answering. The people who are answering don't know. You will spend forty minutes on a call that ends with we'll have more clarity soon, which means what it always means.

What to do instead:

Document what you own. Not defensively. Factually. The projects, the systems, the institutional knowledge that lives only in your head. Write it down. Not because you're preparing to leave. Because clarity about your actual function is the most useful thing you can have when someone new is deciding what to keep.

Watch who is in the meetings. Not the announced meetings. The unannounced ones. The ones that appear on calendars with no agenda and a small, specific attendee list. I've been here eighteen years and those meetings are where the structure gets decided before the structure gets announced.

## How to Read the New Org Chart

The new org chart will be distributed as a PDF. It will be too small to read at 100% zoom.

Count the layers between you and the person who can approve a budget. If that number has increased, your function has been deprioritized regardless of what the announcement said. If it has decreased, something was consolidated and you may now have more visibility than before, which is not always better.

Look at who your new manager's manager is. That person's priorities will become your priorities within ninety days. Not officially. Structurally.

Look for the roles that don't appear on the chart at all. Those are either being filled or being eliminated. The difference won't be communicated until it's been decided. The timeline for that decision is TBD.

## The Job Security Question

Someone will ask HR directly. Someone always does.

The answer will be: We are committed to our people and will communicate as soon as we have more information.

I wrote that down the last time. It is a sentence that contains no information. It is structured to sound like reassurance while committing to nothing. The word committed is doing the work that a specific answer would otherwise do.

The honest version of that sentence is: we don't know yet, or we know and we're not telling you yet. Both are fine. Neither is what gets said.

If you are considering whether to look externally, the reorg is a reasonable moment to update your materials. Not because the outcome is certain. Because the outcome is uncertain, and [twelve applications and nine silences](https://www.corphardcore.com/blog/applied-to-twelve-positions) is a better data set than zero applications and one assumption.

I documented what that process looks like. It is not fast. It is not encouraging. It is information, which is more than the org chart provides.

## What Most People Get Wrong

Most people treat a reorg as an event. It is not an event. It is a condition.

The announcement is the beginning of a process that will take six to nine months to stabilize. New managers arrive with their own priorities. Old processes get renamed. Some get eliminated. A new colleague appears without a desk but already with calendar access, and within three weeks is running a standing sync that didn't exist before. I documented [that specific situation](https://www.corphardcore.com/blog/the-new-colleague) separately.

The people who navigate reorgs without losing their jobs are not the ones who performed visibility in the first week. They are the ones who identified what the new structure actually needed and made themselves useful to that need before anyone asked.

That is not a strategy. It is an observation about what I have watched work, over eighteen years, across several reorgs at the same company.

The ones who performed visibility are mostly gone. I'm still here. I'm not sure what that proves.

## FAQs

**What does a corporate reorg usually mean for employees?**
Reporting lines are changing, some roles are being eliminated, and the remaining headcount will absorb additional responsibilities. The announcement will not say this directly. The org chart will.

**How do you know if your job is at risk during a reorg?**
Watch for your role's absence from the new org chart, a significant increase in the layers between you and decision-making authority, and whether your manager's name appears in the new structure at all. If your manager's role has been eliminated, your function is being evaluated.

**Should you ask HR directly about your job security during a reorg?**
You can. The answer will be a version of we are committed to our people and will communicate when we have more information. That answer is not useful. The useful information comes from the org chart, the meeting invites, and who is and isn't in the room.

**How long does a corporate reorg take to stabilize?**
Six to nine months before the new structure functions as described. Reporting lines shift, priorities change, new managers arrive with their own agendas. The announcement date and the stabilization date are not the same date.

**Is it worth updating your resume during a reorg?**
Yes. Not because the outcome is certain, but because the outcome is uncertain. Having current materials is information. It does not commit you to anything. It gives you options that waiting does not.

**What is the most common mistake people make during a reorg?**
Treating it as a single event with a defined end date. A reorg is a condition that evolves over months. The people who adjust to what the new structure actually needs, rather than performing loyalty to the old one, tend to remain.

**What should you document during a reorg?**
Document what you own: the systems, the projects, the institutional knowledge that exists only because you've been there long enough to hold it. Not as a negotiating tactic. As a record. Clarity about your actual function is more useful than any amount of visibility performance.
